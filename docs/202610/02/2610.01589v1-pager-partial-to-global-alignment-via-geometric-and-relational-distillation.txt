ABSTRACT
Pretrained 3D encoders are typically developed on globally reconstructed scenes
expressed in a consistent world coordinate frame, whereas embodied systems must
reason from partial, viewpoint-dependent observations in camera coordinates. We
show that this shift from globally learned 3D feature spaces to realistic partial ob-
servations exposes a severe representation mismatch, which we find consistently
across representative state-of-the-art encoders, including Sonata and Concerto. A
frozen Sonata encoder with a global linear probe achieves 72.47 mIoU on full
ScanNet scenes, but only 2.57 mIoU on single-frame camera-coordinate inputs.
Training-free gravity alignment recovers performance to 41.64 mIoU, showing
that coordinate-frame mismatch is a dominant source of degradation but cannot
be fully resolved through canonicalization alone. We introduce PAGER, a label-
free adaptation method that aligns partial-view features with a frozen global 3D
semantic space using only paired partial/global geometry. It learns lightweight
adaptation modules while keeping the pretrained encoder and global segmenta-
tion probe frozen. Matched-point feature alignment anchors partial features to
their global counterparts, while relational supervision preserves their similarity
structure with respect to the global representation. Global geometry provides su-
pervision only during training. Inference operates directly on the partial obser-
vation. Without partial-view labels, PAGER outperforms label-supervised PEFT
on both Sonata and Concerto, and in zero-shot ScanNet→ScanNet++ transfer sur-
passes fully fine-tuned Sonata (53.93 vs. 48.09 mIoU), suggesting that preserving
the frozen global representation can improve cross-dataset transfer.

INTRODUCTION
Large-scale 3D representation learning has significantly advanced semantic scene understanding,
enabling pretrained point-cloud encoders to transfer effectively to downstream tasks such as seman-
tic/instance segmentation, and scene parsing (Qi et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024;
Schult et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025). Most existing models, however, are developed and evaluated on globally reconstructed
scenes, where geometry is aggregated across views in a consistent world-coordinate frame (Dai
et al., 2017; Yeshwanth et al., 2023). This setting differs from realistic embodied perception, where
an agent observes only a partial point cloud from its current viewpoint, with incomplete geometry,
occlusions, limited FOV, and camera-coordinate inputs (Fig. 1). Similar viewpoint gaps have been
studied in egocentric and ego-exo video representation learning (Grauman et al., 2022; 2023; Luo
et al., 2025), but their effect on pretrained 3D point-cloud representations remains underexplored.
We show that this gap is not merely a consequence of missing geometry, but a severe representa-
tion mismatch between globally learned 3D feature spaces and view-constrained partial observa-
tions. Using Sonata (Wu et al., 2025) as a strong representative pretrained encoder, we find that a
frozen encoder with a global linear probe reaches 72.47 mIoU on full ScanNet scenes, but drops to
2.57 mIoU on single-frame camera-coordinate partial views. Notably, when the same partial views

This indicates that camera-coordinate partial inputs can lose compatibility with the semantic feature
space learned from globally reconstructed scenes. Recent progress in monocular and multi-view 3D
reconstruction (Wang et al., 2024b; 2025a;b) further motivates this setting, as view-constrained 3D
observations are becoming increasingly available from casually captured visual input.
A straightforward solution is to fine-tune the pretrained encoder on labelled camera-view point
clouds fully. This means updating the full encoder backbone together with a segmentation head
using dense semantic supervision on the target partial-view training split. While this can be ef-
fective in-domain, it is computationally and storage expensive since each target setting requires an
independently adapted model. It also depends on dense semantic annotations of partial 3D obser-
vations, which are costly to obtain, and may reduce cross-dataset generalization when only limited
labelled views are available. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) reduces the number of up-
dated parameters (Houlsby et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2022; Li & Liang, 2021; Zaken et al., 2022),
and geometry-aware methods such as GEM (Tang et al., 2025) have shown that lightweight spatial
adaptation can be effective for 3D scene segmentation. However, existing PEFT methods rely on
semantic supervision in the target domain and have not been applied to partial-view segmentation,
where label biases and missing scene context further limit their effectiveness.
To address this limitation, we propose PAGER, a label-free partial-to-global adaptation framework
that maps partial camera-coordinate observations back into the semantic feature space of a frozen
globally pretrained model. Our key premise is that adapting individual features independently is
insufficient: a partial observation should recover not only the global feature associated with the
same physical point, but also its relationships to the broader global representation.
We therefore derive two complementary forms of supervision entirely from paired partial/global
geometry. First, matched-point feature anchoring aligns each partial feature with the global feature
of the corresponding physical point. Second, a point-specific relational objective preserves how that
feature relates to reference features sampled from the global scene. This transfers structure from
the complete-scene representation without requiring semantic annotation of the partial observations.
The pretrained encoder and global segmentation probe remain frozen throughout adaptation. Paired
global geometry is used only during training. At inference, PAGER operates directly on partial
camera-coordinate point clouds and requires neither the complete scene nor camera pose.
We evaluate PAGER under a partial-view semantic segmentation protocol constructed from stan-
dard 3D scene datasets, including ScanNet and ScanNet++ (Dai et al., 2017; Yeshwanth et al., 2023).

RELATED WORK
3D representation learning. Point-based and transformer-based architectures have driven rapid
progress in 3D semantic segmentation (Neshev et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2021; Wu
et al., 2024; Schult et al., 2023). In parallel, self-supervised pretraining has become the main route
to transferable 3D features (Xiao et al., 2023). PointContrast introduced correspondence-based pre-
training on aligned RGB-D observations (Xie et al., 2020). Masked Scene Contrast and GroupCon-
trast scaled scene-level pretraining and improved semantic grouping (Wu et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
2024a); and Sonata explicitly addressed geometric shortcut learning to produce stronger frozen rep-
resentations (Wu et al., 2025). However, these models are trained and evaluated primarily on glob-
ally reconstructed scenes. Our focus is the deployment gap that arises when such representations are
applied to single-view partial observations.
Egocentric and cross-view understanding. Cross-view learning has been studied extensively in
video through ego/exo datasets and view-invariant representation learning (Grauman et al., 2022;
2023; Luo et al., 2025). In contrast to video, where viewpoint invariance is an established objective,
the analogous gap in pretrained 3D point-cloud representations has received little attention. At
the same time, recent 3D reconstruction systems such as DUSt3R, VGGT, and PI3 (Wang et al.,
2024b; 2025a;b) make dense partial observations from monocular capture readily available, making
this gap increasingly consequential. Dense per-point semantic segmentation from a single partial
camera-coordinate cloud, without multi-frame fusion or reconstruction, remains largely unexplored.
Efficient adaptation and PEFT for 3D. Standard PEFT methods: adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019),
LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), prefix tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), and BitFit (Zaken et al., 2022), are
effective in language and vision but transfer poorly to point clouds, as they adapt at the per-point
level or inject static global tokens, ignoring local spatial structure (Tang et al., 2025). GEM (Tang
et al., 2025) addresses this with geometry-aware adapters, achieving performance close to full fine-
tuning with a small fraction of trainable parameters, but operates in the fully reconstructed-scene
setting and relies on semantic supervision.
In contrast, PAGER targets a more challenging deployment setting: adapting a pretrained global
3D encoder to view-constrained point clouds in camera coordinates, where both scene context and
the coordinate frame differ from pretraining. Rather than relying on semantic labels, PAGER learns
a lightweight translator from paired partial/global geometry alone. Its self-distillation objective
transfers both local and scene-level structure by matching similarity relations to shared global keys,
addressing the combined effects of coordinate mismatch and limited context in local views.

REPRESENTATION GAP ANALYSIS
Before introducing PAGER, we quantify the mismatch between globally pretrained 3D encoders
and view-constrained partial observations. The goal is to isolate why the standard frozen-transfer
recipe fails, and what the adaptation module needs to correct.
Evaluation conditions. We construct four input conditions that progressively increase deployment
realism and evaluate them in world and camera coordinate frames (Fig. 2). Global is the scene-level
3


point cloud accumulated from all frames, corresponding to the conventional training and evaluation
setting for 3D scene understanding. Room Slice retains one sixth of the global point cloud, ob-
tained by partitioning the scene into six slices from the room center. It introduces coarse geometric
incompleteness while preserving the world coordinate frame. View Crop is a spatial crop of the
global point cloud centered at a single camera viewpoint, retaining only points within the region a
single frame would observe while keeping full geometric fidelity within the crop. Single Frame
contains the 3D points back-projected from one RGB-D observation, reflecting realistic deployment
with limited field of view, missing geometry, and depth noise.
For the World variants, all points are represented in the global world coordinate system. For the
Camera variants, points are instead represented in a camera coordinate system. Importantly, only
the Camera—Global variant (Fig. 2, 5thcol.) transforms the entire accumulated scene into the co-
ordinate frame of the first image. This setting is purely a diagnostic experiment. This first-frame
transformation is not used in our main method or main experiments. The remaining Camera vari-
ants, View Crop and Single Frame (Fig. 2, 6th, 7thcol.), do not use the first-frame coordinate system.
Instead, each observation is represented in the coordinate frame of its corresponding camera pose.
Two separable sources of degradation. Table 1 reveals an asymmetry. Within world coordinates,
performance decreases gradually as inputs become more geometrically incomplete: from 72.47
mIoU on Global down to 67.45 mIoU on Single Frame, a drop of under 5 points across four condi-
tions spanning the full range from complete to single-frame reconstruction. (This 67.45 mIoU value
uses ground-truth camera pose to transform Single Frame inputs into world coordinates, an oracle
reference not available to PAGER at inference.) The pretrained representation is more robust to
missing geometry when the coordinate frame is preserved. The coordinate frame tells a completely
different story. Expressing the full Global scene in camera coordinates, with no geometry removed
at all, already collapses mIoU from 72.47 to 5.14. Adding single-frame partiality brings it to 2.57.
The coordinate frame alone, applied to a geometrically complete scene, causes a 67-point drop. Ge-
ometry reduction, by contrast, causes only a 5-point drop. Within the camera-coordinate setting,
geometry reduction continues to degrade performance (5.14 →2.57 mIoU), confirming that both
factors contribute, with the coordinate frame being the larger and qualitatively distinct effect.
Figure 2: Two sources of degradation. Left: progressive geometric reduction (Global →Room
Slice →View Crop →Single Frame) in world coordinates lowers performance due to missing
context. Right: the same inputs with camera coordinates causes a larger drop, visible in both seg-
mentation predictions (Tab. 1) and PCA feature visualizations (bottom row). Coordinate-frame
mismatch is the dominant factor, geometry reduction further compounds the gap. Coordinate sys-
tem origin shown by black/red arrows.
Why does the coordinate frame matter? The Camera—Global condition applies a single rigid
transform to an otherwise unchanged complete scene. The 67-point drop therefore cannot be at-
tributed to missing scene content. The pretrained encoder often relies on world-frame priors (floors
below, gravity-aligned axes) that are disrupted under camera coordinates. Standard augmentations
could not cover all possible variations introduced by camera frames, leaving this gap unaddressed.
Can training-free canonicalization solve the gap? The strong coordinate-frame effect raises the
question of whether the problem can be addressed without learning. We therefore evaluate two
training-free canonicalization strategies. PCA-based axis alignment improves the frozen model from
2.57 to 8.38 mIoU. Gravity alignment, using pitch and roll estimated from a single RGB image with
Perspective Fields (Jin et al., 2023), provides a substantially stronger recovery to 41.64 mIoU.
However, both remain well below PAGER at 65.46 mIoU. Coarse orientation recovery is therefore
helpful but insufficient to restore the frozen global representation. We treat the remaining failure as
a representation mismatch and directly adapt partial-view features toward the global feature space.

METHODOLOGY
Let f be a frozen 3D encoder pretrained on globally reconstructed scenes, producing per-point
features in Rd, and let W ∈RC×d be the frozen linear probe trained on world-coordinate global
scenes for semantic segmentation.
Our goal is to adapt features extracted from partial camera-coordinate observations so that they be-
come compatible with the global feature space learned by f and the frozen classifier W, without
updating the backbone or requiring semantic labels for the target partial views. Adaptation is driven
entirely by correspondences to the pretrained global feature space rather than by class-specific super-
vision. At inference, the model receives only a partial camera-coordinate point cloud and requires
neither the complete scene nor camera pose.
Limitations of Supervised Adaptation. Supervised adaptation is effective in-domain but carries
costs: (i) full fine-tuning updates the entire encoder per target setting, requiring dense partial-view
labels and risking corruption of the global relational structure learned during pretraining (ii) label-
supervised PEFT (e.g., GEM) is parameter-efficient but inherits class taxonomy (iii) pointwise fea-
ture regression is label-free but constrains each adapted feature only relative to its own target, leav-
ing inter-feature relationships unconstrained. PAGER requires no semantic labels, treats every point
without class assumptions, and constrains inter-feature structure rather than only pointwise targets.

4.3
WHY THE TWO LOSSES ARE COMPLEMENTARY
The cosine loss anchors each partial feature to its global target but says nothing about how features
relate to each other. The relational loss fills this gap: by matching similarity patterns over shared
keys, it constrains inter-feature structure rather than just absolute positions. Tab. 4 (A1) shows the
two provide complementary gains.
Why random keys? An alternative is to sample keys from the anchor’s neighbourhood (e.g., k-
NN in world coordinates), but this is counterproductive: spatially adjacent points lie on similar
surfaces and produce highly correlated inner products with the anchor. Random sampling avoids
this by drawing keys broadly across the scene, while remaining O(M) and trivially parallelizable,
in contrast to alternatives such as farthest-point sampling whose cost scales poorly with scene size.
Tab. 4 (A2) confirms the predicted drop when k-NN keys replace random keys.

EXPERIMENTS
We report results on two pretrained encoders, Sonata (Wu et al., 2025) and Concerto (Zhang et al.,
2025), and use Sonata for the ablation studies.
An additional frozen-transfer comparison with
PTv3 (Wu et al., 2024) is in App. A.5.
Datasets. We use ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017), ScanNet200 (Rozenberszki et al., 2022), and Scan-
Net++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023). ScanNet contains 1513 RGB-D scans with 20 and 200 class point-
level annotations. Each frame is back-projected using its captured camera pose, so the evaluation
set spans ScanNet’s natural pose distribution(App. A.1). We sample every 100th frame for train-
ing (∼0.3 FPS, ≈19K frames) and every 30th frame for evaluation (∼1 FPS, ≈18K frames). Scan-
Net++ provides high-fidelity scans with 100 classes. We sample every 60th frame for evaluation
(∼1 FPS, ≈7K frames). All methods use the same subsets for fair comparison. Full statistics in
App. C.3.
Baselines. We compare against methods with increasing label and parameter cost. We evaluate two
PEFT baselines: Adapter (Houlsby et al., 2019), which insert small trainable modules before the
classifier, and GEM (Tang et al., 2025), a geometry-aware PEFT method that adds spatial and context
adapters to the frozen encoder and trains them with supervised cross-entropy. We also include
Sonata (linear), which trains a linear classifier on frozen partial-view features, the standard protocol
for evaluating pretrained 3D representations (Wu et al., 2025). Sonata (late blocks + linear), which
additionally unfreezes the final encoder blocks and Sonata (full + linear), which updates the entire
encoder. All baselines require semantic labels, while PAGER does not.
Metrics. We report mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy.
Additional implementation details are provided in App. B.2.
5.1
RESULTS
In-domain adaptation. On ScanNet, PAGER outperforms label-supervised PEFT on both encoders
without partial-view labels (Tab. 2), reaching 65.46 mIoU with Sonata and 71.67 with Concerto ver-
sus 59.81 and 69.90 for GEM (Tang et al., 2025). Linear probing and adapter-based PEFT (Houlsby
et al., 2019) fail to recover (36.17–41.49 mIoU). The gains are larger in mAcc (80.50 vs. 73.92
on Sonata, 87.77 vs. 80.96 on Concerto), and on Sonata PAGER closes most of the remaining
gap to full fine-tuning (65.46 vs. 69.26 mIoU) with only 1.2% of the trainable parameters. On the
finer-grained taxonomies, PAGER outperforms GEM with Concerto on both ScanNet++ (33.97 vs.
32.86) and ScanNet200 (26.73 vs. 25.06). With Sonata it trails GEM (27.38 vs. 33.27 and 19.25
vs. 21.97) but remains above all other supervised PEFT baselines. Qualitative results are shown in
Fig. 4 and per-class results in App. A.3.
Backbone generality. Sonata (Wu et al., 2025) and Concerto (Zhang et al., 2025) differ substantially
in pretraining: point-only self-supervision versus joint 2D-3D self-supervision. Yet both collapse on
partial camera-coordinate input (2.57 and 4.97 mIoU, Tab. 2), indicating that the representation gap
identified in Sec. 3 is a property of global pretraining rather than of a particular encoder. PAGER
uses the same adapter and objectives for both and requires only dense per-point features, with no
encoder-specific components. That the stronger Concerto representation yields consistent gains over
7


supervised GEM across all three benchmarks suggests PAGER benefits directly from improvements
in the underlying pretrained encoder.
Cross-domain evaluation. To assess robustness to distribution shifts, we train PAGER and all
baselines exclusively on ScanNet, using only ScanNet partial/global geometry pairs for PAGER’s
self-distillation, and evaluate on ScanNet++ without further adaptation. No ScanNet++ scenes, ge-
ometry, or labels are seen during training. We map predictions to the 20 classes used for training. As
shown in Tab. 3, PAGER learns features that are more resilient to domain changes, achieving higher
performance when training and testing data come from different distributions. Full fine-tuning is
commonly treated as an upper bound, and on ScanNet Partial it is. That bound, however, is local
to the source domain: it costs 108.5M updated parameters and dense partial-view labels per setting.
Under distribution shift to ScanNet++, this specialisation becomes a liability, the fully fine-tuned
model loses 21 mIoU relative to its in-domain score and falls behind PAGER (53.93 vs. 48.09
mIoU), which never modified the backbone.

ANALYSIS EXPERIMENTS
A1: The two losses are complementary. The cosine anchor and relational distillation target distinct
aspects of the partial-to-global gap (Sec. 4.3). Tab. 4 (A1) confirms this empirically: Lcos alone
reaches 63.31 mIoU on ScanNet Partial, already strong, since matched-point supervision transfers
local class identity well, while LKL alone reaches 55.58 mIoU, consistent with the prediction that
relational signatures are insufficient when the absolute class direction is unconstrained. Combining
the two lifts performance to 65.46 mIoU (+2.15 over cosine-only, +9.88 over KL-only), confirming
the two terms capture different aspects of the alignment.
A2: Random keys outperform k-NN keys. Tab. 4 (A2) shows the predicted direction: replac-
ing random sampling with k-NN-based key selection causes a substantial drop from 65.46 to 49.71
mIoU on ScanNet Partial, in line with the intuition that broadly sampled keys provide a more infor-
mative target distribution than spatially clustered ones.
A3: Number of keys. The key set size M controls the breadth of the relational reference: too few
keys cannot span the scene, too many are redundant. Performance is stable across two orders of
magnitude, varying by under 1.5 mIoU, and we adopt M = 4096 as the best value found in this
sweep rather than a sharply defined optimum. Since keys are sampled at random, the cost is linear
in M and independent of scene size.
Comparison with D-DITR. We additionally compare with Distilled DINO In The Room (D-
DITR) (Knaebel et al., 2026), which distills 2D vision foundation model features into a 3D backbone
for semantic segmentation (Tab. 5). We apply publicly available D-DITR checkpoints trained on
ScanNet-20 and ScanNet-200 directly to partial-view inputs, using both original ScanNet partial
scenes and their RGB-D reconstructed counterparts (2.5D). Since D-DITR was not designed for
camera-coordinate partial-view inference, the large gap in (Tab. 5) primarily reflects this distribu-
tion shift rather than a direct algorithmic comparison. We include it to show that 2D-to-3D feature
distillation in global coordinates does not transfer trivially to our partial-view setting.

CONCLUSION
We presented PAGER, a label-free framework that aligns partial camera-coordinate 3D features
with a frozen global semantic space using only paired geometry and no semantic labels. Our analysis
isolates coordinate-frame mismatch as the dominant source of degradation in frozen 3D transfer, and
show that geometric self-distillation, combining a matched-point cosine anchor with a point-specific
relational objective, sufficiently repairs this mismatch without modifying the backbone. PAGER
outperforms label-supervised PEFT in-domain and surpasses fully fine-tuned Sonata in zero-shot
cross-dataset transfer, using only 1.3M trainable parameters across two distinct pretrained encoders.

A.4
RELATIONAL LOSS PARAMETERIZATION
The relational objective constrains each partial feature’s similarity pattern to a set of global reference
keys (Sec. 4.2). Tab. 11 compares two parameterizations of this constraint: KL divergence between
softmax distributions, and direct regression of the sampled similarities.
Both improve substantially over the cosine anchor alone, and the choice between them is a trade-off
rather than a ranking. Gram regression gives higher mIoU (+1.81 on Sonata, +2.47 on Concerto)
and higher allAcc, while KL gives higher mAcc (+2.75 and +2.51 respectively). Since mAcc
weights all classes equally regardless of frequency, the KL formulation is preferable when per-class
balance matters, and Gram regression when overall segmentation quality is the target. We use KL
throughout the main paper as our originally validated configuration. That the pattern is consistent
across two architecturally distinct encoders supports our claim that the benefit derives from point-
specific relational supervision rather than from a specific divergence.

Additionally to our experiments with Sonata and Concerto encoders, we compare the Sonata encoder
with PTv3 (Wu et al., 2024), another widely used 3D backbone. Sonata is the strongest pretrained
representation in our setting, as shown in Tab. 12. Since PTv3 uses a different decoder, we also
evaluate Sonata with the PTv3 decoder for a controlled comparison. Sonata performs best in our
Sonata (frozen) setup, so we use it for all main experiments.

correspondence are discarded.

C.1
CORRESPONDENCES BETWEEN PARTIAL POINT CLOUDS AND THE GLOBAL MESH
The same world-space coordinates are also use to construct the voxel-level correspondences used in
our loss. For each scene, we perform a nearest-neighbor search in the global space and assign each
original partial point cloud coordinate to its closest global scene original coordinate.
To ensure these correspondences remain valid after augmentation, we explicitly track how every
point moves throughout the entire transform chain. At the beginning of preprocessing, each point
is assigned a persistent origin identifier, and after every point-indexing operation we update the
mapping that links the current points back to their original positions. During voxelization, we
additionally record which original points correspond to each voxel representative, allowing us to
propagate origin-level correspondences into voxel-level correspondences that remain aligned with
the subsequent augmentations.
C.2
MOBILE ROBOT DATA ACQUISITION
To assess PAGER under real-world conditions, we collect partial point clouds using a Clearpath
Husky A200 UGV equipped with a Roboception RC-Visard 160c stereo camera mounted on a rigid
front-facing tower. The camera provides synchronized left/right RGB images and onboard SGM-
based disparity and depth maps, which we use exclusively.
Camera intrinsics are obtained through Roboception’s built-in calibration tool, and the stereo system
outputs rectified images, so no additional geometric calibration is required. We back-project the
stereo depth into 3D using the calibrated intrinsics to form partial point clouds.
Points beyond 7m are discarded to remove distant background measurements. Surface normals are
estimated with (Bae & Davison, 2024).
C.3
DATASET STATISTICS
Datasets. We employ three widely used indoor 3D datasets: ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017), Scan-
Net200 (Rozenberszki et al., 2022) and ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023): (1) ScanNet is col-
lected using a lightweight RGB-D scanning setup and comprises 1513 indoor scenes, covering ap-
proximately 2M views, with corresponding 3D camera poses, surface reconstructions, and point-
level semantic annotations for 20 classes and 200 classes (for the ScanNet200 version respectively).
For training all methods we sample every 100th frame from the RGB-D sequences (∼0.3 FPS) of
the training set, resulting in approximately 19K training frames. This higher sampling stride was
chosen to ensure training efficiency given the large number of available frames. For evaluation we
sample every 30th frame (∼1 FPS) from the validation set, resulting in about 18K evaluation frames.
(2) We also use ScanNet++, which comes with high-fidelity 3D mask annotations, including smaller
objects (100 classes). It includes high-resolution 3D scans captured at sub-millimeter precision and
annotated comprehensively, covering objects of varying sizes.

C.4
BROADER IMPACTS
Reliable partial-view 3D segmentation supports applications such as robotics, AR/VR, and assistive
navigation, where embodied agents must reason from limited onboard observations. As with any
indoor scene understanding system, deployment in human-centred environments raises privacy and
monitoring considerations that warrant care in real-world use.
